Chapter 12 · On the Arc Between the Tropics
===========================================

*Almagest* Book I, Chapter 12 (:cite:`Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 64–68 <heiberg:1898>`)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. picture:: /_static/figures/fig08_solar_declination_quadrant.svg

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0074
   :page: p. 64

Now that the magnitudes of the straight lines in a circle have been set forth, the first thing, as we said, would be to demonstrate how much the oblique circle through the middle of the zodiacal signs is inclined to the equator—that is, what ratio the great circle through both of the aforesaid poles has to the arc of it intercepted between the poles, which is clearly equal to the distance from the equator to either of the solstitial points. This is immediately apprehended by us instrumentally through a simple construction of the following sort.

We shall make a bronze circle of moderate size, accurately turned with a square cross-section, which we shall use as a meridian ring by dividing it into the assumed :math:`360` parts of the great circle, and each of these into as many subdivisions as possible. Then, fitting a second thinner ring inside the aforesaid ring so that their faces remain in a single plane while the smaller ring can rotate unobstructedly inside the larger in the same plane toward north and south, we shall attach at two diametrically opposite divisions of the smaller ring, along one of its faces, two small

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0075
   :page: p. 65

equal prisms pointing straight toward each other and toward the center of the rings, placing along the middle of their width fine pointer-needles touching the face of the larger, graduated ring. Fitting this securely, for each observation, upon a pillar of suitable size and setting the base of the pillar in the open air upon a foundation level with the plane of the horizon, we shall take care that the plane of the rings is perpendicular to the plane of the horizon and parallel to that of the meridian. The former of these is achieved by means of a plumb-line suspended from the point that is to be at the zenith, watched until by adjusting wedges underneath it directs its alignment to the diametrically opposite point; and the latter is achieved by drawing a clearly marked meridian line on the plane beneath the pillar and turning the rings sideways until their plane is sighted parallel to that line. Once this placement is accomplished, we observed the northward and southward excursion of the Sun by rotating the inner ring at noon until the lower prism was completely shaded by the upper one.

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0076
   :page: p. 66

And when this occurred, the tips of the pointer-needles indicated to us how many degrees from the zenith the center of the Sun stood on each occasion along the meridian.

Even more conveniently, we carried out this same observation by constructing, instead of the rings, a square, unwarped plinth of stone or wood having one of its faces flat and accurately smoothed, upon which—taking a point near one of the corners as center—we inscribed a quadrant of a circle, joined from the point at the center to the inscribed arc the straight lines enclosing the right angle under the quadrant, and similarly divided the arc into :math:`90` degrees and their subdivisions. After this, along one of the straight lines—the one destined to stand perpendicular to the plane of the horizon and face south—we inserted two small, identical, smoothly turned cylinders perpendicular and equal in all directions, one precisely centered upon the point at the center and the other near the lower end of the

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0077
   :page: p. 67

straight line. Then, setting this inscribed face of the plinth alongside a meridian line drawn on the underlying plane so that it also held a position parallel to the plane of the meridian, and making the straight line through the cylinders plumb and perpendicular to the plane of the horizon by means of a plumb-line suspended across the cylinders—correcting any slight deficiency again with thin wedges—we likewise observed at noon the shadow cast by the cylinder at the center, placing an object against the inscribed arc so that its position appeared more clearly; and marking the midpoint of that shadow, we read off the corresponding division of the quadrant arc, clearly indicating the latitudinal passage of the Sun along the meridian.

From such observations, then—and especially those scrutinized by us right around the solstices themselves over several cycles, since the reading at both the summer and the winter solstices generally intercepted the same and equal divisions of the meridian circle from the zenith point—we found that the arc from the northernmost limit to the southernmost, which is the arc between the tropics,

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0078
   :page: p. 68

always came out to be greater than :math:`47\frac{2}{3}` degrees and less than :math:`47\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{4}` degrees (:math:`47\frac{3}{4}^\circ`), from which virtually the same ratio is deduced as that of :person:`Eratosthenes <eratosthenes>`, which :person:`Hipparchus <hipparchus>` also adopted; for the arc between the tropics comes out to very nearly :math:`11` parts of those of which the meridian is :math:`83`.

From the same observation, the latitudes of the places where we conduct observations are also readily obtained: we take the midpoint between the two limits, which lies on the equator, and the arc between that point and the zenith point, which is obviously equal to the elevation of the poles above the horizon.
