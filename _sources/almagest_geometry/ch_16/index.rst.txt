Chapter 16 · On Risings on the Right Sphere

*Almagest* Book I, Chapter 16 (:cite:`Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 82–85 <heiberg:1898>`)

Next it will be fitting to demonstrate also the magnitudes of the arcs of the equator cut off by the circles described through its poles and given segments of the oblique circle; for thus we shall have in how many equatorial time-degrees the segments of the zodiacal circle cross both the meridian everywhere and the horizon on the right sphere, because the horizon too is then alone described through the poles of the equator.

Let, then, the previously demonstrated figure be set out, and with arc :math:`EH` of the oblique circle given first as :math:`30` degrees, let it be required to find arc :math:`E\Theta` of the equator.

In the same way as before, the ratio of the chord subtending double arc :math:`ZB` to the chord subtending double arc :math:`BA` is compounded of the ratio of the chord subtending double arc :math:`ZH` to the chord subtending double arc :math:`H\Theta` and the ratio of the chord subtending double arc :math:`\Theta E` to the chord subtending double arc :math:`EA`. Now double arc :math:`ZB` is :math:`132^\circ 17' 20''` and its chord is :math:`109^p 44' 53''`, while double arc :math:`BA` is :math:`47^\circ 42' 40''`

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0093
   :page: p. 83

and its chord is :math:`48^p 31' 55''`. Again, double arc :math:`ZH` is :math:`156^\circ 40' 1''` and its chord is :math:`117^p 31' 15''`, while double arc :math:`H\Theta` is :math:`23^\circ 19' 59''` and its chord is :math:`24^p 15' 56''`. If therefore from the ratio of :math:`109^p 44' 53''` to :math:`48^p 31' 55''` we remove the ratio of :math:`117^p 31' 15''` to :math:`24^p 15' 56''`, there will remain to us the ratio of the chord subtending double :math:`\Theta E` to the chord subtending double :math:`EA`, namely the ratio of :math:`54^p 52' 26''` to :math:`117^p 31' 15''`; and the same ratio is also that of :math:`56^p 1' 25''` to :math:`120^p`. Now double :math:`EA` is :math:`180^\circ`, and the chord subtending it is :math:`120^p`; therefore the chord subtending double :math:`\Theta E` is :math:`56^p 1' 25''` of the same parts. Consequently double arc :math:`\Theta E` will be :math:`55^\circ 40'` approximately, and :math:`\Theta E` itself will be :math:`27^\circ 50'` of the same degrees.

Again, let arc :math:`EH` be supposed :math:`60^\circ`, so that, the rest remaining the same, double arc :math:`ZH` becomes :math:`138^\circ 59' 42''` and its chord :math:`112^p 23' 56''`, while double arc :math:`H\Theta` becomes :math:`41^\circ 0' 18''` and its chord :math:`42^p 1' 48''`. If therefore from the ratio of :math:`109^p 44' 53''` to :math:`48^p 31' 55''` we remove the ratio of :math:`112^p 23' 56''` to :math:`42^p 1' 48''`, there will remain the ratio of the chord subtending double :math:`\Theta E` to the chord subtending

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0094
   :page: p. 84

double :math:`EA`, namely the ratio of :math:`95^p 2' 40''` to :math:`112^p 23' 56''`; and the same ratio as this is also that of :math:`101^p 28' 20''` to :math:`120^p`. Now the chord subtending double arc :math:`EA` is :math:`120^p`; consequently the chord subtending double :math:`\Theta E` is :math:`101^p 28' 20''` of the same parts. Therefore double arc :math:`\Theta E` will be :math:`115^\circ 28'` approximately, and :math:`\Theta E` itself will be :math:`57^\circ 44'` of the same degrees.

Thus it has been demonstrated that the first twelfth-part (zodiacal sign) of the ecliptic from the equinoctial point corresponds in time, in the manner set forth, to :math:`27^\circ 50'` of the equator, and the second to :math:`29^\circ 54'`, since both together were proved to be :math:`57^\circ 44'`; and clearly the third sign will correspond in time to the remaining :math:`32^\circ 16'` completing the quadrant, because the entire quadrant of the oblique circle corresponds in time to the entire quadrant of the equator with respect to the circles described through the poles of the equator.

Following the same method as the preceding demonstration, we have also computed the arcs of the equator corresponding in time to each ten-degree segment of the oblique circle, since segments smaller than these do not differ appreciably from differences of uniform increase. We shall therefore set these out as well, so that we may have ready at hand in how many time-degrees each

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0095
   :page: p. 85

of them crosses both the meridian everywhere, as we said, and the horizon on the right sphere, taking our start from the ten-degree segment at the equinoctial point.

The first, then, encompasses :math:`9^\circ 10'` time-degrees, the second :math:`9^\circ 15'`, and the third :math:`9^\circ 25'`, so that in total the :math:`27^\circ 50'` time-degrees of the first sign are collected; the fourth encompasses :math:`9^\circ 40'`, the fifth :math:`9^\circ 58'`, and the sixth :math:`10^\circ 16'`, so that the :math:`29^\circ 54'` time-degrees of the second sign are collected; the seventh encompasses :math:`10^\circ 34'`, the eighth :math:`10^\circ 47'`, and the ninth :math:`10^\circ 55'`, so that again the :math:`32^\circ 16'` time-degrees of the third sign (at the solstitial points) are collected, and the :math:`90^\circ` of the whole quadrant in agreement.

And it is immediately evident that the order of the remaining quadrants is also the same, since all the same relations occur in each of them because the sphere is supposed right—that is, the equator is uninclined to the horizon.
