Section 3 · Inclined Great Circles & Ecliptic Diameters
=======================================================

*Planisphaerium*, Section 3 (:cite:`Heiberg 1907, Vol. II, pp. 231–232 <heiberg:1907>`)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. picture:: /_static/figures/fig09_planisphaerium_stereographic_astrolabe.svg

Intersection of the Horizon and Zodiac (`Heiberg II, p. 231`)
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

.. folio:: heiberg_1907_opera_minora_vol2:0441
   :page: p. 231

We next designate another circle inclined to the equinoctial circle in place of the horizon, such that it bisects only the equinoctial; whence it is necessary that the two points where this circle and the zodiac intersect each other be potentially opposite across a diameter; by which I mean that the line joining those points passes through the center of the equinoctial.

For let the equinoctial circle, as we are accustomed, be :g:`abgd <cABGD>` around center :g:`e <E>`, and the zodiac circle :g:`hbtd <cMBTD>`, whose points of intersection with the equator are joined by diameter :g:`bed <BD_ast>`. Let the horizon :g:`hatg` bisect the equinoctial into equal halves along diameter :g:`aeg <MK>`, and let the common intersection of the horizon and the zodiac be at points :g:`h` and :g:`t <T>`.

**I say that** if a straight line connects point :g:`h` with center :g:`e <E>` in place of the meridian circle and is produced in a straight line, it will necessarily pass through point :g:`t <T>`.

Demonstration via Intersecting Chords (`Heiberg II, p. 232`)
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

.. folio:: heiberg_1907_opera_minora_vol2:0442
   :page: p. 232

.. picture:: 0442.g1.png
   :alt: Folio 0442 diagram (archival scan)

.. picture:: 0442.g1.svg
   :alt: Folio 0442 diagram (interactive vector)

*Demonstration.* Let the straight line :g:`he` be drawn, and let it go straight until it strikes the horizon, and let it strike it at point :g:`t <T>`.

**I say that** point :g:`t <T>` is also common to the circle of the zodiac.

For since in circle :g:`hatg` two lines :g:`ag <MK>` and :g:`ht` cut each other, the rectangle contained by :g:`ae` and :g:`eg` is equal to the rectangle contained by :g:`he` and :g:`et` (by :just:`Eucl. III.35 <III.35>`):

.. math::

   ae \cdot eg = he \cdot et.

Yet :g:`ae` and :g:`eg` correspond to :g:`be` and :g:`ed`, so it is also equal to the rectangle contained by :g:`be` and :g:`ed`:

.. math::

   be \cdot ed = he \cdot et.

Whence, by the converse of :just:`Eucl. III.35 <III.35>`, it is necessary that :g:`bd <BD_ast>` and :g:`ht` lie in one and the same circle.

Wherefore it follows that point :g:`t <T>` is marked upon the zodiac. But :g:`t <T>` was also marked upon the horizon, which is circle :g:`hatg`; and the straight line :g:`th` joins their common intersection, which is established to pass through the center :g:`e <E>` of the equinoctial.

Whence it is manifest that **the zodiac is none the less cut by the horizon at points opposite across a diameter**.
