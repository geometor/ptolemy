Section 8 · Rising of the Signs in the Right Sphere: First Sign¶

Planisphaerium, Section 8 (Heiberg 1907, Vol. II, pp. 236–240)¶

With these matters settled, it is next fitting to measure the quantity of the rising of the signs, as it occurs in the corporeal sphere. For let

f. 0447 · p. 237

there be, as usual, the equinoctial circle abgd around center e, and the zodiac zbhd around center t, with the diameters drawn orthogonally through e in place of the meridian circle, one joining the points of intersection b and d, which are also the equinoctial points, and the other passing through both centers, gh and az, whose tropic points are h and z.

Since, therefore, our intention is to demonstrate how much of the equinoctial circle rises in the right sphere with any given degrees of the zodiac, and since the position of the horizon in the right sphere is like that of the meridian circle—namely in the power of straight lines passing through the pole of the equinoctial circle, point e, which is the position of the meridian circle— it is established that since arcs zb and hd are quadrants of the oblique circle, they rise with arcs ab and gd, quadrants of the equinoctial circle, and culminate and set equally with them. Now since line bd bisects the semidiameter th in circle abgd orthogonally at point e, two equal arcs must be cut off from the zodiac, namely bk and dl. Therefore lines kmen

f. 0448 · p. 238

and lcey are drawn through.

This done, since parallel circles pass through points k, l and y, n, whose distance on either side from the equinoctial circle is equal, so that point k is potentially opposite to point n and likewise point l to point y, if we set arc bk as the sign of Pisces, ld will be the sign of Libra, and in the same manner by in place of Aries and dn in place of Virgo.

Therefore, when line ktl is drawn through, since triangle kte is equal in both sides and angles to triangle lte, angle ket will also be equal to angle let, and likewise the remaining angles keb and led, and those opposite to them. Since these are at the center of the equinoctial circle, the arcs of that same circle under these angles, which rise with each of these signs, must also be equal; of which it suffices to investigate the quantity of one, say bm, to measure the rising of each.

Accordingly, we drop the perpendicular tf onto line ke. This done, since out of those parts of which \(60^p\) contain the semidiameter of the equinoctial circle, \(65^p 36' 17''\) measure line tk, the semidiameter of the zodiac, \(26^p 31' 58''\) measure line et between the centers of the circles, and \(73^p 39' 7''\) measure line ek, the semidiameter of the parallel circle designated at the beginning of Pisces and the beginning of Scorpio—namely points k and l—triangle tke is known. Therefore, if we apply the square on tk, minus the square on te, to line ke, the excess of line kf over line ef will be determined.

For as often as the greater of two mutually intersecting circles bisects the lesser,

f. 0449 · p. 239

if the square of the distance of the centers be subtracted from the square of the greater semidiameter, there remains the square of the semidiameter of the lesser circle. Here, therefore, since the oblique circle bisects the equinoctial in this manner, the square on the semidiameter tk of the greater exceeds the square on te, the distance of the centers, by as much as the semidiameter eb of the lesser produces from itself, since angle bet is right and line tb is equal to line tk.

Since, moreover, \(60^p\) parts measure line eb, the semidiameter of the equinoctial circle, its square must contain \(3600^p\) of those same parts, while from the foregoing \(73^p 39' 7''\) measure line ek; and if we apply that difference—namely the square on eb—to this line, there results the excess of line kf over line fe, which is \(48^p 52' 42''\). When this is subtracted from line ke, there remain \(24^p 46' 25''\), half of which—namely \(12^p 23' 12''\)—will measure line fe, out of those parts of which \(26^p 31' 58''\) measure line et.

Therefore, out of those parts of which there are \(120^p\) in line et, which subtends the right angle eft, there must be numbered in line fe nearly \(55^p 59'\), and the arc of chord fe must measure \(55^\circ 40'\) out of the \(360^\circ\) of the whole circle circumscribing right triangle fet.

f. 0450 · p. 240

Therefore, out of the \(360^\circ\) in four right angles, angle fte will contain \(27^\circ 50'\). This angle together with angle fet equals a right angle, which itself likewise completes a right angle with angle bek; therefore, subtracting the common intermediate angle, angle bek remains equal to angle fte, so \(27^\circ 50'\) measure angle bek. And since this is at the center of the equinoctial circle, arc bm subtending it must also measure \(27^\circ 50'\) out of the \(360^\circ\) of the whole equinoctial circle. These, therefore, are the degrees and minutes—as laid down in the corporeal sphere—of the equinoctial circle with which the four signs placed around the equinoctial points rise in the position of the fixed sphere.

Yet we can also arrive at this by an easier way. For as ke is in en, so is be in ed; now be in ed is \(3600^p\), which when divided by line ek yields line en, so it is established to be known. And since ke exceeds this by twice line fe, it follows that fe is likewise known. Moreover et is also known; and since it is opposite the right angle at f, angle fte—namely the angle equal to keb—will be known, from which follows the knowledge of arc bm.
