Section 10 · Rising of the Signs in the Oblique Sphere: General Theorem¶

Planisphaerium, Section 10 (Heiberg 1907, Vol. II, pp. 241–244)¶

With these things thus confirmed, we must next examine whether the rising of the signs in the oblique sphere itself is the same, or whether a different

f. 0452 · p. 242

method is required than the one established in the right sphere. We shall therefore follow the manner of the example given in the book of the Almagest concerning the parallel passing through the island of Rhodes, to whose horizon the northern pole rises by \(36^\circ\), whose semidiameter, as was established among the things stated above, is measured by \(102^p 4' 45''\), and the distance of its center from the equinoctial center by \(82^p 35' 3''\).

Accordingly, let the equinoctial circle, as is customary, be abgd around center e, and the zodiac zbhd around center t. This done, we understand the motion of the sphere—as though about the north pole fixed at point e—to proceed from point d through points g and b to point a. Let us therefore first understand of these horizon circles two arcs passing equally through both tropic points, which are z and h, namely zkhl and zmhn. It is established, therefore, that when the position of the horizon is as arc zkhl lies, point z and point k necessarily rise together and points h and l opposite to them set at the same moment; whereas when it is as arc zmhn lies,

f. 0453 · p. 243

conversely, that is, points n and h rise together and at the same hour m and z set, so long as the motion of the sphere is understood such as we have assigned, namely with the north pole fixed at mark e.

These things being settled, since—as was stated above—not only the zodiac bisects the equinoctial, but every horizon also bisects both the latter and the former, when we have marked them in this manner, the straight lines joining the points of intersection, kl and mn, must pass through center e; from which it is clear that arc gl is equal to arc ka and likewise arc am is equal to arc gn. It remains that arc am be also established equal to arc ak. We shall therefore fix the two centers of the horizon according to these arcs at point c and point y, and draw lines ct and ty, and likewise ce and ye. Since, therefore, whenever two circles intersect each other, if the line joining their centers be produced to cut the line joining the points of intersection, it must bisect it orthogonally, it follows that cty is one straight line bisecting line zh orthogonally. No differently, ce is perpendicular to kl and likewise ye is perpendicular to mn. Therefore the two triangles around et between c and y are equal in both sides and angles, as they correspond to one another—namely angle cet to angle yet. Moreover, angles yem and cek are equal as being right; whence it follows that the remaining angles, namely aem and aek, are also equal, and likewise arcs am and ak

f. 0454 · p. 244

are manifestly equal, and likewise lg and gn, and each of the latter to each of the former.

Since, therefore, arc hb rises with arc nb, and likewise arc bz with arc bk, which is equal to bn, and again arc zd with arc kd and arc dh with arc dn, which is equal to dk, from these things it is established that arcs of the oblique circle that stand at equal distances on either side from the equinoctial points rise in equal quantity. Furthermore, since arc bz decreases from its rising in the right sphere by the quantity of arc ka, while the opposite arc dh increases by as much as arc gn—namely equal to ka—and h is the summer tropic point, it is established that the signs around the vernal equinox decrease from their right-sphere rising by as much as those opposite them exceed their rising; whence it follows that the shortest day of that clime is less than the equinoctial day by as much as both arcs ak and gn make up, and the longest day greater by as much.
