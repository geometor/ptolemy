Section 20 · General Conclusion on the Construction of the Planisphere¶

Planisphaerium, Section 20 (Heiberg 1907, Vol. II, pp. 258–259)¶

In this manner, therefore, it is fitting that the construction in the planisphere be made of the circles in the corporeal sphere—namely those found by reason of the equinoctial circle, those parallel to it, and also the meridian circles, as well as those found by reason of the zodiac, those parallel to it, and also the circles of the horizon. In such a construction, the pole of the equinoctial circle holds the place of the center both for the right circle itself and for all circles parallel to the right circle; which rule compels the northern ones always to be smaller and the southern ones always larger, the former decreasing as in the sphere, but the latter increasing in reverse order to the sphere, and likewise extending all meridians into straight lines. Yet the pole of the zodiac is center neither for itself nor for any parallel to it; whence it happens that one of them is without a center—that is, it becomes a straight line. In the great circles passing through this pole it is otherwise; for those passing through both poles become straight lines, on which the centers of the unequal parallels to the zodiac are located. Whence in the assignment of stars, whichever way the position be made—by relation to the equinoctial circle or by relation to the zodiac—in both we divide both the

equinoctial and the zodiac; yet if it be by relation to the equinoctial, we divide along with it the parallels to it likewise, and if by relation to the zodiac, with it also those parallel to it. Whichever way is done assigns the position of the stars; yet the surest thing among these is that in either mode it be brought into agreement with what takes place in the corporeal sphere; namely, once those found on account of the equinoctial circle have been determined, let those employed on account of the zodiac be made according to an example as close as possible to Egypt. Nor is it necessary to execute everything in the planisphere, observing only circles passing through every two or three or even six degrees in a medium position— which numbers are common measures of the \(30^\circ\) of each sign and of the roughly \(24^\circ\) of distance between the equinoctial and each tropic point, until they meet with the tropic circles themselves and with the meridian circles distinguishing the signs.
