Composed at Alexandria by Claudius Ptolemy and translated into Latin in 1143 by Hermann of Carinthia, the Planisphere (Planisphaerium) is the foundational ancient text establishing the mathematical geometry of stereographic projection and the planispheric astrolabe. Each section of this Multi-Track Book Codex provides synchronized reading tracks (English, Latin, Summary, and Notes) wired to interactive diagrams.

Section 1 · Notes

Unlike the Almagest, no Greek manuscript of Ptolemy's Planisphaerium (Ἅπλωσις ἐπιφανείας σφαίρας) has survived. Translated into Arabic in ninth-century Baghdad and revised with astrolabe chapters around 1000 CE in Córdoba by Maslama al-Majrīṭī, the work entered Latin Europe when Hermann of Carinthia (Hermannus Secundus) completed his Latin version at Toulouse on 1 June 1143 (dedicated to Thierry of Chartres).

Section 2 · Notes

The Astrolabe Tympan: In classical and medieval astrolabes, removable plates (tympana or safā’ih) were engraved for specific terrestrial latitudes $\varphi$. The dominant circular arc on each plate is the local Horizon (circulus finitor), separating the visible upper hemisphere from the invisible lower hemisphere. Section 2 provides the mathematical proof that drawing the horizon circle through the East and West equatorial points and its meridian intersections accurately separates the spherical sky.

Section 2 · Summary

Horizon Representation on the Astrolabe: - The local horizon for observer latitude $\varphi$ is an inclined great circle on the celestial sphere whose pole is the Zenith. - Like the oblique ecliptic, the projected horizon cuts the equatorial plane in an eccentric circle whose center lies along the local meridian line.

Section 3 · Notes

Euclid III.35 as an Inversive Invariant: Ptolemy’s reliance on Eucl. III.35 ($ae \cdot eg = he \cdot et$) illustrates how classical Greek geometers handled the power of a point with respect to a circle. In the modern theory of circle inversion (developed by Steiner, Möbius, and Plücker in the 19th century), this property is expressed by stating that the point $e$ has constant power $-R^2$ with respect to every circle representing an inclined great circle on the sphere.

Section 3 · Summary

Circle-Intersection Power of a Point: - In circle $hatg$ (the horizon), chords $ag$ and $ht$ intersect at center $e$. - By Euclid III.35, $ae \cdot eg = he \cdot et$. - Since $a, b, g, d$ are on the equatorial circle around center $e$, the equatorial radii satisfy $ae = be = eg = ed = R_{\text{eq}}$. - Therefore $be \cdot ed = he \cdot et$.

Section 4 · Notes

Explicit Cross-Reference to the *Almagest*: Ptolemy’s explicit phrase on page 234, line 16—quemadmodum in Almagesti constitutum est (“as was established in the Almagest”)—provides crucial internal evidence directly linking the Planisphaerium to the Syntaxis Mathematica. In the Arabic and medieval Latin traditions, this citation cemented the complementary status of the two works: the Almagest providing spherical astronomy and tabular parameters, and the Planisphaerium providing the practical planar geometry for instrument makers.
