Composed at Alexandria by Claudius Ptolemy and translated into Latin in 1143 by Hermann of Carinthia, the Planisphere (Planisphaerium) is the foundational ancient text establishing the mathematical geometry of stereographic projection and the planispheric astrolabe. Each of the twenty sections of this Multi-Track Book Codex provides synchronized reading tracks (English, Latin, Summary, and Notes) wired to Heiberg’s 1907 critical witness.

Section 1 · Stereographic Projection of Equator, Tropics & Ecliptic

Since it is possible, O Syrus (Jesure), and frequently necessary, that the circles occurring upon the solid sphere be represented in a flat plane just as if it actually were flat, it seemed advisable in the truth of science that one who wishes to know this should set forth a demonstrating rationale whereby it is proper to assign the oblique circle, the circles parallel to the equinoctial circle, and the circles known through the meridian circle, and whatever is intended to be adapted to that which appears in the corporeal sphere.

Section 20 · General Conclusion on the Construction of the Planisphere

In this manner, therefore, it is fitting that the construction in the planisphere be made of the circles in the corporeal sphere—namely those found by reason of the equinoctial circle, those parallel to it, and also the meridian circles, as well as those found by reason of the zodiac, those parallel to it, and also the circles of the horizon. In such a construction, the pole of the equinoctial circle holds the place of the center both for the right circle itself and for all circles parallel to the right circle; which rule compels the northern ones always to be smaller and the southern ones always larger, the former decreasing as in the sphere, but the latter increasing in reverse order to the sphere, and likewise extending all meridians into straight lines. Yet the pole of the zodiac is center neither for itself nor for any parallel to it; whence it happens that one of them is without a center—that is, it becomes a straight line. In the great circles passing through this pole it is otherwise; for those passing through both poles become straight lines, on which the centers of the unequal parallels to the zodiac are located.
