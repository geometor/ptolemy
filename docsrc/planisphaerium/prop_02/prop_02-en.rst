Section 2 · The Horizon & Division of Equator and Zodiac
========================================================

*Planisphaerium*, Section 2 (:cite:`Heiberg 1907, Vol. II, pp. 230–231 <heiberg:1907>`)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. picture:: /_static/figures/fig09_planisphaerium_stereographic_astrolabe.svg

The Horizon as an Inclined Great Circle (`Heiberg II, p. 230`)
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

:line:`p. 230` Every horizon will next be designated just as we designated the oblique circle—not only because it bisects the equinoctial circle, but also because it potentially bisects the zodiac; by which I mean that it must be designated through parts that potentially correspond to those diametrically opposite on the corporeal sphere.

For let the equinoctial circle be described as before with letters :g:`a, b, g, d <cABGD>` around center :g:`e <E>`, and the oblique circle with letters :g:`z, b, h, d <cMBTD>` bisecting the equinoctial at points :g:`b <B>` and :g:`d <D>`. We then draw through pole :g:`e <E>` in place of the meridian circle a straight line in any direction, say through :g:`z, a, e, h, g <MK>`.

**I say that** the points :g:`z <DZ>` and :g:`h <lineDH>` correspond to points diametrically opposite on the sphere; by which I mean that circles parallel to the right circle designated at these points cut off equal arcs on either side of the equinoctial circle, in the manner we set forth, just as if it were on the sphere itself.

Demonstration from Orthogonal Rays (`Heiberg II, pp. 230–231`)
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

*Demonstration.* That this may be established, let there rise from point :g:`e <E>` a straight line perpendicular to :g:`ag <MK>` up to the circumference at point :g:`t <T>`; straight lines are then drawn, :g:`tz <lineDH>` and :g:`ta <MK>`, and likewise :g:`th <arcGH>` and :g:`tg <MK>`.

Since angle :g:`∠atg` is in a semicircle, it is established to be a right angle (:just:`Eucl. III.31 <III.31>`).

:line:`p. 231` Furthermore, since the rectangle contained by :g:`ze` and :g:`eh` is equal to the square on :g:`ed <BD_ast>`, and :g:`ed = et <ET>`, it is also equal to the square on :g:`et <ET>`. Whence it is necessary that as :g:`ze` is to :g:`et <ET>`, so is :g:`et <ET>` to :g:`eh`; therefore angle :g:`∠zth` is also a right angle.

Angle :g:`∠atg` having been shown to be right, subtracting the common middle angle leaves angle :g:`∠atk` necessarily equal to angle :g:`∠gtl`; whence it follows that arc :g:`ak` and arc :g:`lg` are equal.

From these things, therefore, since lines :g:`tk` and :g:`tl` apply to arcs having the same distance from point :g:`d <D>` on the equinoctial circle—which, drawn from point :g:`t <T>` equally distant from opposite quadrant points :g:`a <MK>` and :g:`g <K>`, strike line :g:`zg` at points :g:`z` and :g:`h`, through which two circles parallel to the right circle must be designated at equal distance on both sides—it is necessary that line :g:`zeh` connect points that potentially terminate a diameter of the oblique circle.
