Since it is possible, O :person:`Syrus <syrus>` (*Jesure*), and frequently necessary, that the circles occurring upon the solid sphere be represented in a flat plane just as if it actually were flat, it seemed advisable in the truth of science that one who wishes to know this should set forth a demonstrating rationale whereby it is proper to assign the oblique circle, the circles parallel to the equinoctial circle, and the circles known through the meridian circle, and whatever is intended to be adapted to that which appears in the corporeal sphere.

This intention requires that in place of the meridian circle we use straight lines, and between the parallel circles to the right circle, that first a great circle be made assigning the oblique circle, reaching on this side and that the parallels to the right circle at equal distance on both sides, which it bisects in this manner:

We describe the equinoctial circle with letters :g:`a, b, g, d <cABGD>` around center :g:`e <E>`, whose diameters orthogonally cutting one another are :g:`ag <MK>` and :g:`bd <BD_ast>`. We shall understand one diameter to be the meridian circle, and point :g:`e <E>` to be the northern pole; nor indeed is it fitting that the other pole be placed in the plane with respect to this one, as will be established in what follows. And since the northern pole perpetually appears in our region, it is more suitable in the plane whose assignment is ours.

Projection of Parallels from the South Pole (`Heiberg II, pp. 227–228`)
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

.. folio:: heiberg_1907_opera_minora_vol2:0438
   :page: p. 228

It is therefore necessary that of the circles parallel to the right circle, the northern parallel be described internally, and the southern parallel externally. That this may be done correctly, we produce line :g:`ag <MK>` in both directions, and from circle :g:`abgd <cABGD>` on both sides of :g:`g <K>` we cut off two equal arcs—above :g:`gh <arcGH>`, and below :g:`gn <arcGN>`. We join straight lines from :g:`d <D>` with both marks, in such manner that :g:`dh <lineDH>` reaches line :g:`ag <MK>` and marks point :g:`k <K>`, while :g:`dn <lineDN>` where it touches line :g:`ag <MK>` marks point :g:`t <T>`.

This being done, with center fixed at :g:`e <E>` at distance :g:`ek <EK>`, a circle is made upon diameter :g:`km <cKM>` (the Tropic of Capricorn); and without moving, another circle is consequently made at distance :g:`et <ET>` of the line upon diameter :g:`tl <cTL>` (the Tropic of Cancer).

Theorem: The Projected Oblique Ecliptic Circle Bisects the Equator (`Heiberg II, pp. 228–230`)
^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^

Then, segment :g:`tm <MK>` being bisected at its midpoint :g:`r <R>`, a circle is described around point :g:`r <R>` with radius equal to the half of :g:`tm <MK>`.

**I say that** those two circles are parallel to the equinoctial at equal distance on both sides, and the third circle around center :g:`r <R>` is the oblique circle, which line :g:`tm <MK>` cuts into equal halves until it reaches both of them—the one at mark :g:`m <M>` and the other at point :g:`t <T>`—**and that it bisects the equinoctial circle, which it intercepts at the two opposite points** :g:`b <B>` **and** :g:`d <D>`.

*Demonstration.* That this may be established by reason, a straight line :g:`dm <MD>` joins :g:`d <D>` with :g:`m <M>`, crossing the equinoctial circle at point :g:`z <Z>`. Since therefore arc :g:`az <arcAZ>` is equal to arc :g:`gh <arcGH>`, which is given equal to arc :g:`gn <arcGN>`, it is necessary that arc :g:`zdn <arcZDN>` be half the whole circle; whence it follows that **angle** :g:`∠mdt <angMDT>` **is a right angle** (by :just:`Eucl. III.31 <III.31>`).

Since therefore the circle described upon line :g:`tm <MK>` circumscribes right-angled triangle :g:`mdt <triMDT>` and passes through point :g:`d <D>`, it must also pass through point :g:`b <B>`; consequently it cuts the equinoctial circle into equal halves.

.. folio:: heiberg_1907_opera_minora_vol2:0439
   :page: p. 229

Hence it is established that between circles parallel to the right circle, when we duplicate equal arcs on either side of point :g:`g <K>`, their quantity measures the arc of total declination. When we join their ends by straight lines with point :g:`d <D>`, we set the straight segments they cut off from line :g:`ek <EK>` as the radii of the circles described around center :g:`e <E>`, by the artifice of the given example—so that internally is the Tropic of Cancer, and externally the Tropic of Capricorn, with the zodiac circle touching both and cutting the equinoctial into equal halves, as has been described.

Our determination measures each of arcs :g:`ng <arcGN>` and :g:`gh <arcGH>` as :math:`23^\circ 51'` (twenty-three parts and nearly fifty-one minutes) out of the :math:`360^\circ` measuring the whole circle :g:`abgd <cABGD>`, which is the equal distance of both tropics from the equinoctial circle.

Accordingly, of the parallel circles on either side, :g:`tl <cTL>` is the summer tropic and :g:`km <cKM>` is the winter tropic; from which it is established that the intermediate circle :g:`mbtd <cMBTD>`, touching the tropic circles—at :g:`t <T>` the summer solstice, and at :g:`m <M>` the winter solstice—bisects the equinoctial, just as if, starting from point :g:`b <B>`, it were carried through :g:`a <MK>` to :g:`d <D>`. On this account it is not fitting that the divisions of the oblique circle be equal arcs, but rather they shall be adapted as in the following example.

By this I mean that we take the beginnings of the zodiacal signs from the points where it intersects the circles parallel to the equinoctial, designated by the method we have taught, at the distance of each sign from the right circle, just as it stands in the corporeal sphere of the circle of signs. By this method, therefore, every
