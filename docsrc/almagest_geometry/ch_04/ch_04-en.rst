Chapter 4 · That the Earth, Taken as a Whole, Is Sensibly Spherical
===================================================================

*Almagest* Book I, Chapter 4 (:cite:`Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 14–16 <heiberg:1898>`)
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0024
   :page: p. 14

That the earth too is spherical to sense perception when taken as a whole, we may best understand in this way:
for again it is possible to see the sun, the moon, and the other stars not rising and setting at the same time for all people on the earth,

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0025
   :page: p. 15

but always earlier for those who dwell toward the east and later for those toward the west.
For we find that eclipse appearances taking place at the same time, and especially lunar eclipses, are not recorded by all observers at the same hours—that is, at hours equidistant from noon—but always the hours recorded by the more easterly observers are later than those by the more westerly.
And since the difference in the hours is found proportional to the distances between the places, one would reasonably suppose the surface of the earth to be spherical, with the uniformity of its curvature taken as a whole always producing the occultations proportionally for successive places;
and if its figure were otherwise, this would not happen, as one might see even from these considerations.

For if it were concave, the stars would appear to rise earlier for the more westerly observers; and if it were plane, they would rise and set simultaneously and at the same time for all on the earth;
and if it were triangular or square or any other polygonal figure, again they would rise and set alike and at the same time for all dwelling on the same straight side, which is never seen to happen.
And that it could not be cylindrical either, so that its curved surface should be turned toward east and west, while the

.. folio:: heiberg_1898_syntaxis_vol1_pt1:0026
   :page: p. 16

sides of its plane bases are turned toward the poles of the cosmos—which some might suppose more plausible—is clear from this:
for no star would be ever-visible to anyone dwelling on the curved surface, but either all stars would both rise and set for all observers, or the same stars equidistant from either pole would be always invisible to all.
Yet as it is, the more we travel toward the bears, the more of the southern stars are hidden and the more of the northern appear, so that it is clear that here too the curvature of the earth, producing occultations proportionally toward the flanks as well, demonstrates its spherical figure from every side;
along with the fact that when we sail toward mountains or any high places from whatever angle and in whatever direction, their heights are observed to increase gradually as though emerging from the sea itself, having previously been submerged owing to the curvature of the surface of the water.
