Chapter 5 · That the Earth Is in the Middle of the Heavens¶

Almagest Book I, Chapter 5 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 16–20)¶

Now that this has been examined, if one were next to consider also the position of the earth, one would perceive that in this way

f. 0027 · p. 17

alone are the phenomena concerning it brought to pass, if we suppose it to lie in the middle of the heavens like the center of a sphere. For if this were not so, then the earth would necessarily either be outside the axis while equidistant from each of the poles, or lie upon the axis while shifted toward one of the poles, or neither lie upon the axis nor be equidistant from each of the poles.

Now against the first of these three positions the following objections contend: if it were conceived as shifted upward or downward for some observers, it would befall them on a right sphere that equinox never occurs, since what is above the earth and what is below the earth are always divided into unequal parts by the horizon; while on an inclined sphere either no equinox would occur at all, or it would not fall in the passage midway between the summer solstice and the winter solstice, since these intervals would necessarily become unequal because the equator, which is the greatest of the parallel circles described around the poles of the revolution, would no longer be bisected by the horizon, but rather one of the circles parallel to it, either farther north or farther south. Yet it is agreed by everyone without exception that these intervals are equal

f. 0028 · p. 18

everywhere, inasmuch as the increases of the longest day at the summer solstice beyond the equinoctial day are equal to the decreases of the shortest days at the winter solstice. And again, if the displacement were supposed toward the east or west parts for some observers, it would befall them too that neither the sizes and distances of the stars appear equal and the same at both the eastern and western horizons, nor would the time from rising to culmination be equal to that from culmination to setting, which things evidently contradict the phenomena altogether.

Now against the second of the positions, according to which it is conceived as on the axis yet shifted toward one of the poles, one might again object that, if this were so, in each clime the plane of the horizon would always make the parts of the heaven above and below the earth differently unequal both in relation to themselves and to one another, since on a right sphere alone can the horizon bisect it, whereas on an inclination that makes the nearer pole always visible, it always diminishes what is above the earth and increases what is below the earth, so that even the great circle through the middle of the zodiac would be divided unequally by the plane of the horizon, which is by no means

f. 0029 · p. 19

observed to be so, since six twelfth-parts are always visible above the earth to everyone while the remaining six are invisible, and then again all of the latter appear together above the earth while the former are simultaneously invisible; so that it is clear that the sections of the zodiac are bisected by the horizon, since the same entire semicircles are intercepted now above the earth and now below the earth.

And in general, if the earth did not lie beneath the equator itself, but inclined northward or southward toward one of the poles, not even to sense perception at the equinoxes would the eastern shadows of gnomons be in a straight line with the western ones upon planes parallel to the horizon, which is manifestly observed to follow everywhere. And it is clear from this very fact that neither can the third of the positions hold, since both sets of objections from the first two would occur in it.

To sum up, the whole order observed in the increases and decreases of days and nights would be utterly confounded if the earth were not set in the middle, and moreover eclipses of the moon could not occur in all parts of the heavens at its diametrical opposition to the sun, since the earth would often intercept them not in

f. 0030 · p. 20

diametrical passages, but at intervals smaller than a semicircle.
