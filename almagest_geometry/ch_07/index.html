Chapter 7 · That the Earth Makes No Translational Motion Either¶

Almagest Book I, Chapter 7 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 21–26)¶

By the same arguments as before it will be shown that the earth cannot make any motion into the aforementioned lateral parts, nor shift away at all from its place at the center; for the same things would occur as if it happened to have a position other than the middle. Consequently, it seems to me superfluous for anyone even to seek the causes of the motion toward the middle, once it is so evident from the phenomena themselves that the earth occupies the middle place of the cosmos and that all heavy things are carried toward it. And this alone would most readily assist such comprehension, that since the earth has been proved spherical and in the middle of the universe, as we have said,

f. 0032 · p. 22

in all its parts without exception, the inclinations and motions of bodies having weight—I mean their proper ones—everywhere and always take place at right angles to the uninclined plane drawn through the point of contact upon impact; for it is clear from this being so that, if they were not checked by the surface of the earth, they would in every case reach the center itself, since the straight line leading to the center is always at right angles to the plane tangent to the sphere through the section at the contact.

Now those who think it paradoxical that so great a weight of the earth neither rests upon anything nor is carried along seem to me to err by making their comparison with reference to their own experiences and not to the property of the whole. For such a thing would no longer seem astonishing to them, I think, if they considered that this size of the earth, compared with the whole surrounding body, has the ratio of a point to it; for thus it will seem possible that the proportionally smallest is held and braced everywhere equally and at equal inclination by the altogether greatest and uniform, since there is no below

f. 0033 · p. 23

or above in the cosmos relative to it, just as no one could conceive such a thing in a sphere either; rather, of the compound bodies within it, so far as their own natural motion is concerned, the light and rarefied are fanned outward toward the circumference, seeming to make their impulse toward what is upward for each observer because for all of us what is overhead, called upward, also inclines toward the enclosing surface, while the heavy and dense are borne toward the middle and center, seeming to fall downward because for all of us what is toward our feet, called downward, also inclines toward the center of the earth and naturally settle about the middle through their equal and similar mutual resistance and counter-thrust from all sides. Thus, reasonably, the whole solid mass of the earth is apprehended as being so exceedingly great relative to the things borne upon it as to remain everywhere utterly unmoved by the impulse of the very smallest weights, and as it were receiving whatever falls upon it. Indeed, if it too had one common motion, the same as the other weights, it would clearly outstrip everything in its downward descent through so great an excess of size, and living creatures and individual

f. 0034 · p. 24

weights would be left behind riding upon the air, while the earth itself would very swiftly have fallen completely out of the heavens themselves. But such notions, even merely conceived, would appear the most ridiculous of all.

Now some people, more plausibly as they think, having nothing to urge against these arguments, assent to them, yet suppose nothing will contradict them if for argument’s sake they assume the heavens motionless and the earth turning about the same axis from west to east nearly one revolution each day, or even if they move both to any extent whatever, provided only it is about the same axis, as we said, and commensurate with their mutual overtaking.

It has escaped them that, as regards the phenomena of the stars, nothing perhaps would prevent this from being so on the simpler view, yet from the events around ourselves and in the air such a notion would be seen to be utterly ridiculous. For even if we should grant them something so contrary to nature—that the finest and lightest things either do not move at all or move no differently from those of the opposite nature, even though things in the air and less fine-grained manifestly make swifter motions than all the more earthy ones, while the

f. 0035 · p. 25

densest and heaviest things make their own swift and uniform motion, even though earthy things are admittedly sometimes not readily moved by other things— still, they would have to admit that the rotation of the earth would be the most violent of all motions around it, as completing so great a circuit in a short time, so that everything not standing upon it would appear always to make one motion opposite to the earth, and neither would a cloud ever be seen passing toward the east nor any other flying or thrown thing, since the earth would always outrun everything and anticipate the eastward motion, so that all other things would seem to fall back and yield toward the west.

For even if they should say that the air is carried around with it in the same manner and at the same speed, nevertheless the composite bodies in it would always seem to fall behind the motion of both together; or even if they themselves were carried around as though united with the air, they would no longer appear either to advance or to fall behind, but would always stay still and make no wandering or

f. 0036 · p. 26

passing in their flights or throws—all of which we see happening so manifestly that no slowness or swiftness whatever attends them from the earth’s not standing still.
