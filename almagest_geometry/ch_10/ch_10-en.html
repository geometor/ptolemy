Chapter 10 · On the Magnitude of Straight Lines in a Circle¶

Almagest Book I, Chapter 10 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 31–46)¶

p. 31 For ease of practical use, then, we shall subsequently construct a tabular exposition of the magnitudes of these chords, dividing the circumference into $360$ segments, setting out the arcs side by side at increments of half a degree, and giving alongside each arc the straight line subtending it when the diameter is divided into $120$ parts—because of the arithmetical symmetry of this number, as will become evident from the calculations themselves.

p. 32 First, however, we shall show how, through the fewest and exactest theorems—and the very same ones throughout—we carry out a rapid and systematic demonstration of their magnitudes, so that we do not merely have the tabular magnitudes of the chords set out nakedly without inspection, but may also manage their verification and proof cleanly through a geometric construction. In general we shall employ the sexagesimal system (ἑξηκοστά) because of the awkwardness of unit fractions, always carrying multiplications and divisions out to yield approximations whose discrepancy does not differ even sensibly from rigorous precision.

1. Golden Decagon & Pentagon Construction (Heiberg I, pp. 32–35)¶

Proposition (Ptolemy’s Golden Decagon & Pentagon Construction). Let there be a semicircle ΑΒΓ upon diameter ΑΔΓ around center Δ, and from Δ let ΔΒ be drawn at right angles (∠ΑΔΒ) to ΑΓ. Let ΔΓ be bisected at Ε (ΔΕ = ΕΓ), let ΕΒ be joined, and let ΕΖ be placed equal to ΕΒ along ΔΑ (via the circular arc ΒΖ centered at Ε), and let ΖΒ be joined.

I say that ΖΔ is the side of the regular decagon, and ΒΖ that of the regular pentagon.

p. 33 Demonstration. Since the straight line ΔΓ is bisected at Ε and another straight line ΔΖ is added to it in a straight line, by Eucl. II.6 the rectangle contained by ΓΖ and ΖΔ, together with the square on ΕΔ, is equal to the square on ΕΖ—that is, to the square on ΒΕ, since ΒΕ = ΕΖ:

$$\Gamma Z \cdot Z\Delta + E\Delta^2 = EZ^2 = BE^2.$$

Yet because angle ∠ΕΔΒ is right, by Eucl. I.47 the squares on ΕΔ and ΔΒ are equal to the square on ΒΕ:

$$E\Delta^2 + \Delta B^2 = BE^2.$$

Subtracting the common square on ΕΔ, the remaining rectangle ΓΖ · ΖΔ is equal to the square on ΔΒ, that is, to the square on ΔΓ:

$$\Gamma Z \cdot Z\Delta = \Delta B^2 = \Delta\Gamma^2.$$

Therefore, as ΓΖ is to ΓΔ, so is ΓΔ to ΔΖ. Consequently ΓΖ has been cut in extreme and mean ratio at Δ (Eucl. VI.Def.3).

Now since the side of the hexagon and the side of the decagon inscribed in the same circle, when placed in a single straight line, cut the whole line in extreme and mean ratio (Eucl. XIII.9), and since ΓΔ being a radius is equal to the side of the hexagon (Eucl. IV.15 Coroll.), therefore ΔΖ is equal to the side of the regular decagon.

And in the same manner, since the side of the regular pentagon has its square equal to the sum of the squares on the side of the hexagon and the side of the decagon inscribed in the same circle (Eucl. XIII.10), and in the right-angled triangle ΒΔΖ the square on ΒΖ is equal to the squares on ΒΔ (the hexagon side) and ΔΖ (the decagon side), therefore ΒΖ is equal to the side of the regular pentagon.

p. 34 Sexagesimal Evaluation of the Primary Chords. Since then, as I stated, we set the diameter of the circle to $120$ parts ($120^p$), it is immediate from what has been established that:

  1. Regular Hexagon ($\theta = 60^\circ$): $\Delta E = \frac{1}{2}\Delta\Gamma = 30^p$ and $\Delta B = 60^p$, while the hexagon chord itself equals the radius:

    $$\operatorname{crd}(60^\circ) = 60;0,0^p.$$

  2. Regular Decagon ($\theta = 36^\circ$): Since $E\Delta^2 = 900$ and $\Delta B^2 = 3600$, we have $EB^2 = EZ^2 = 4500$, whence $EZ \approx 67;4,55^p$ in sexagesimals. Subtracting $E\Delta = 30^p$, the decagon side ΔΖ is:

    $$\operatorname{crd}(36^\circ) = 30(\sqrt{5} - 1) \approx 37;4,55^p, \qquad \Delta Z^2 \approx 1375;4,15.$$

  3. Regular Pentagon ($\theta = 72^\circ$): Adding $\Delta B^2 = 3600$ to $\Delta Z^2 = 1375;4,15$ gives ΒΖ² $= 4975;4,15$, whose square root yields the pentagon side ΒΖ:

    $$\operatorname{crd}(72^\circ) = 30\sqrt{10 - 2\sqrt{5}} \approx 70;32,3^p.$$

p. 35 4. Inscribed Square & Equilateral Triangle ($\theta = 90^\circ, 120^\circ$): Instantly from the center ($R^2 = 3600$), the square on the $90^\circ$ chord is $2R^2 = 7200$ and the square on the $120^\circ$ chord is $3R^2 = 10800$:

$$\operatorname{crd}(90^\circ) = 60\sqrt{2} \approx 84;51,10^p, \qquad \operatorname{crd}(120^\circ) = 60\sqrt{3} \approx 103;55,23^p.$$

  1. Supplementary Chords ($180^\circ - \theta$): Since the sum of the squares on any chord and on the chord subtending the remainder of the semicircle equals the square on the diameter ($D^2 = 14400$, by Eucl. III.31 and Eucl. I.47), subtracting $\operatorname{crd}^2(36^\circ) = 1375;4,15$ from $14400$ leaves $13024;55,45$, whence $\operatorname{crd}(144^\circ) \approx 114;7,37^p$; and similarly $\operatorname{crd}(108^\circ) \approx 97;4,56^p$.

2. Ptolemy’s Theorem on Cyclic Quadrilaterals (Heiberg I, pp. 36–37)¶

p. 36 Having obtained these primary chords, the next step is to set forth the corollary that follows directly through the theorem we are about to write down, being most useful on every occasion:

Proposition (Ptolemy’s Theorem). Let there be any quadrilateral inscribed in a circle, ΑΒΓΔ, and let its diagonals ΑΓ–Γ and ΒΔ be joined. We must prove that the rectangle contained by ΑΓ and ΒΔ is equal to the sum of the rectangles contained by the opposite sides, ΑΒ · ΔΓ and ΑΔ · ΒΓ.

Construction. Let angle ∠ΑΒΕ be set out equal to angle ∠ΔΒΓ by drawing ΒΕ to meet diagonal ΑΓ at Ε (Eucl. I.23).

p. 37 Demonstration. If to each of the equal angles ∠ΑΒΕ and ∠ΔΒΓ we add the common angle ∠ΕΒΔ, then the whole angle ∠ΑΒΔ is equal to the whole angle ∠ΕΒΓ. Yet angle ∠ΒΔΑ is also equal to angle ∠ΒΓΕ, for they stand upon the same circumference ΑΒ (Eucl. III.21). Therefore triangle ΑΒΔ is equiangular with triangle ΕΒΓ! Consequently, by Eucl. VI.4, their sides about the equal angles are proportional—as ΒΓ is to ΓΕ, so is ΒΔ to ΔΑ:

$$\frac{B\Gamma}{\Gamma E} = \frac{B\Delta}{\Delta A} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B\Gamma \cdot \Delta A = B\Delta \cdot \Gamma E \qquad\text{(}:just:`Eucl. VI.16 <VI.16>`\text{)}.$$

Again, since angle ∠ΑΒΕ is equal to angle ∠ΔΒΓ, and angle ∠ΒΑΕ is equal to angle ∠ΒΔΓ (subtending the same arc ΒΓ, Eucl. III.21), triangle ΑΒΕ is equiangular with triangle ΔΒΓ. Therefore proportionally, as ΒΑ is to ΑΕ, so is ΒΔ to ΔΓ:

$$\frac{BA}{AE} = \frac{B\Delta}{\Delta\Gamma} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad BA \cdot \Delta\Gamma = B\Delta \cdot AE \qquad\text{(}:just:`Eucl. VI.16 <VI.16>`\text{)}.$$

Now it was proved above that $B\Gamma \cdot \Delta A = B\Delta \cdot \Gamma E$. Adding the two equalities together and invoking Eucl. II.1 ($B\Delta \cdot AE + B\Delta \cdot \Gamma E = B\Delta \cdot A\Gamma$), the whole rectangle contained by $A\Gamma$ and $B\Delta$ is equal to the sum of the rectangle $AB \cdot \Delta\Gamma$ and the rectangle $A\Delta \cdot B\Gamma$. Which was to be proved.

3. Chord of the Difference (Heiberg I, p. 38)¶

p. 38 Corollary (Difference of Two Arcs). Given a semicircle ΑΒΓΔ on diameter ΑΔ, suppose the chords ΑΒ and ΑΓ from Α are given in magnitude. Joining ΒΓ, ΒΔ, and ΓΔ, the supplementary chords ΒΔ and ΓΔ are immediately given via Eucl. III.31 and Eucl. I.47 ($\operatorname{crd}^2(180^\circ - \theta) = D^2 - \operatorname{crd}^2\theta$). Since ΑΒΓΔ is a cyclic quadrilateral in the circle, Ptolemy’s Theorem gives:

$$AB \cdot \Gamma\Delta + A\Delta \cdot B\Gamma = A\Gamma \cdot B\Delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad B\Gamma = \frac{A\Gamma \cdot B\Delta - AB \cdot \Gamma\Delta}{A\Delta}.$$

Applying this immediately to the pentagon arc ($72^\circ$) and hexagon arc ($60^\circ$), Ptolemy obtains the chord of their difference ($12^\circ$):

$$\operatorname{crd}(12^\circ) = \frac{\operatorname{crd}(72^\circ)\operatorname{crd}(120^\circ) - \operatorname{crd}(60^\circ)\operatorname{crd}(108^\circ)}{120} \approx 12;32,36^p.$$

4. Half-Arc Chord Lemma (Heiberg I, pp. 39–42)¶

p. 39 Proposition (Half-Arc Chord). Let there be a semicircle ΑΒΓ on diameter ΑΓ, let chord ΓΒ be given, and let arc ΓΒ be bisected at Δ (arc ΓΔ = arc ΔΒ). Let ΑΒ, ΒΔ, ΓΔ, and ΑΔ be joined, and from Δ let perpendicular ΔΖ be dropped onto ΑΓ.

I say that ΓΖ is equal to half the excess of the diameter ΑΓ over chord ΑΒ, and that GammaDelta2AGammacdotGammaZ.

p. 40 Demonstration. Cut off ΑΕ = ΑΒ along ΑΓ and join ΔΕ. Since $AB = AE$, $A\Delta$ is common, and angle $B A\Delta = E A\Delta$ (subtending equal arcs $B\Delta$ and $\Delta\Gamma$), triangle ΑΒΔ is congruent to triangle ΑΕΔ (Eucl. I.4). Therefore base ΒΔ = ΕΔ, and since $B\Delta = \Gamma\Delta$, we have ΔΕ = ΔΓ.

In the isosceles triangle ΔΕΓ, the perpendicular ΔΖ bisects the base ΕΓ (Eucl. I.26); hence frac12frac12.

In right triangle ΑΔΓ (right-angled at $\Delta$ by Eucl. III.31), altitude ΔΖ to hypotenuse ΑΓ yields by Eucl. VI.8:

$$\frac{A\Gamma}{\Gamma\Delta} = \frac{\Gamma\Delta}{\Gamma Z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma\Delta^2 = A\Gamma \cdot \Gamma Z = 60 \cdot (120 - \operatorname{crd}(180^\circ - \theta)).$$

p. 41 Through successive bisections of the $12^\circ$ chord, Ptolemy extracts:

  • $\operatorname{crd}(6^\circ) \approx 6;16,49^p$

  • $\operatorname{crd}(3^\circ) \approx 3;8,28^p$

  • $\operatorname{crd}(1\frac{1}{2}^\circ) \approx 1;34,15^p$

  • $\operatorname{crd}(\frac{3}{4}^\circ) \approx 0;47,8^p$

p. 42 Ptolemy notes an essential limitation of classical planar geometry: finding the chord subtending one-third of a given arc (trisection of $1\frac{1}{2}^\circ$ into $\frac{1}{2}^\circ$) can by no means be achieved by means of lines (οὐδαμῶς γίνεται διὰ τῶν γραμμῶν).

5. Ptolemy’s Inequality Lemma & Squeeze of $\operatorname{crd}(1^\circ)$ (Heiberg I, pp. 43–46)¶

p. 43 To overcome the impossibility of angle trisection, Ptolemy establishes his famous inequality lemma:

Lemma (Ratio of Unequal Chords). In any circle, the ratio of a greater chord to a lesser chord is strictly less than the ratio of the corresponding arcs:

$$\frac{\operatorname{crd}\alpha}{\operatorname{crd}\beta} < \frac{\alpha}{\beta} \qquad (\text{for } \alpha > \beta).$$

Demonstration. In circle ΑΒΓΔ, let chords ΒΓ > ΑΒ. Bisect angle ∠ΑΒΓ by ΒΔ, cutting chord ΑΓ at Ε. Since ΒΔ bisects the angle, by Eucl. VI.3:

$$\frac{\Gamma E}{EA} = \frac{\Gamma B}{BA}.$$

p. 44 Because $BC > AB$, point $E$ lies closer to $A$ than to $\Gamma$. Dropping perpendicular ΔΖ onto ΑΓ and drawing arcs around $\Delta$, Ptolemy demonstrates through sector inequalities that:

$$\frac{\Gamma E}{EA} < \frac{\operatorname{arc}\Gamma B}{\operatorname{arc} BA} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\operatorname{crd}\alpha}{\operatorname{crd}\beta} < \frac{\alpha}{\beta}.$$

p. 45–46 The Squeeze on $1^\circ$:

  1. Since $1^\circ < 1\frac{1}{2}^\circ$, the lemma yields $\frac{\operatorname{crd}(1\frac{1}{2}^\circ)}{\operatorname{crd}(1^\circ)} < \frac{1\frac{1}{2}}{1} = \frac{3}{2}$, whence:

    $$\operatorname{crd}(1^\circ) > \frac{2}{3}\operatorname{crd}(1\frac{1}{2}^\circ) = \frac{2}{3}(1;34,15^p) = 1;2,50^p.$$

  2. Since $\frac{3}{4}^\circ < 1^\circ$, the lemma yields $\frac{\operatorname{crd}(1^\circ)}{\operatorname{crd}(\frac{3}{4}^\circ)} < \frac{1}{\frac{3}{4}} = \frac{4}{3}$, whence:

    $$\operatorname{crd}(1^\circ) < \frac{4}{3}\operatorname{crd}(\frac{3}{4}^\circ) = \frac{4}{3}(0;47,8^p) = 1;2,50\frac{2}{3}^p.$$

Because both upper and lower bounds agree to seconds at $1;2,50^p$, Ptolemy definitively establishes:

$$\operatorname{crd}(1^\circ) \approx 1;2,50^p.$$

Applying the half-arc lemma to $1^\circ$, he obtains the fundamental table step:

$$\operatorname{crd}\left(\frac{1}{2}^\circ\right) \approx 0;31,25^p.$$
