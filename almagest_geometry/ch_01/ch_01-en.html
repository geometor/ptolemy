Chapter 1 · Preface¶

Almagest Book I, Chapter 1 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 4–8)¶

Those who have genuinely pursued philosophy, O Syrus, seem to me to have done very well in separating the theoretical part of philosophy from the practical. For even if it happens that the practical part is itself previously theoretical, one would nevertheless find a great difference between them, not only because some of the moral virtues can belong to many even without instruction, whereas it is impossible to attain the theory of the universe without teaching, but also because the greatest benefit accrues there from continual activity in the things themselves, and here from progress in the theorems. Hence we have thought it fitting for ourselves

f. 0015 · p. 5

to regulate our actions in the very applications of our impressions, so that not even in ordinary matters do we forget the examination leading to that noble and well-ordered disposition, and to devote most of our leisure to the teaching of theorems, many and noble as they are, and especially of those properly called mathematical. For indeed Aristotle very fittingly divides the theoretical part into the three primary genera: the physical, the mathematical, and the theological. For since all existing things have their being from matter, form, and motion—none of which can be observed separately in its substrate, though each can be conceived alone without the others—if one takes the first cause of the first motion of the universe in its simplicity, one would consider it an invisible and unmoved god, and would call the kind that investigates this theological, since such an activity can only be conceived somewhere above, about the loftiest parts of the cosmos, and is utterly separated from perceptible substances; while the kind that investigates material and ever-moving quality, occupying itself with white, hot, sweet, soft, and the like, one would call physical, since such substance moves for the most part in corruptible things and beneath the lunar sphere; while the kind that exhibits quality with respect to forms and local motions,

f. 0016 · p. 6

being inquisitive of figure, number, magnitude, and furthermore place, time, and the like, one would define as mathematical, since such substance falls as it were between those two, not only because it can be conceived both through sense perception and without sense perception, but also because it belongs to all existing things without exception, both mortal and immortal: changing together with those that are ever changing with respect to their inseparable form, while for eternal beings of the aethereal nature preserving unmoved the immutability of their form. Reflecting upon these things—that one might call the other two genera of the theoretical part conjecture rather than scientific knowledge, the theological because it is altogether unseen and ungraspable, the physical because of the instability and obscurity of matter, so that for this reason one could never hope that philosophers will agree about them—whereas the mathematical alone, if one approaches it inquiringly, would furnish sure and unshakeable knowledge to those who practice it, inasmuch as its demonstration proceeds by indisputable ways, arithmetic and geometry, we have been led to cultivate as far as possible all such theory, and especially that conceived regarding divine and heavenly things, as this alone occupies itself with the examination of things that are always and identically constituted,

f. 0017 · p. 7

and for this reason is itself able, with respect to its proper object of knowledge—which is neither obscure nor disorderly—to remain always and identically constituted, which is the mark of science, and to cooperate with the other branches no less than they themselves do. For this branch can best prepare the way for the theological kind, since it alone can well conjecture concerning the unmoved and separate activity from its proximity to the attributes of substances that are perceptible—both moving and moved, yet eternal and impassible—regarding the revolutions and orders of their motions. And toward the physical branch it would make no ordinary contribution; for nearly every general property of material substance is revealed from the distinctive character of its local motion: thus the corruptible and the incorruptible from rectilinear and circular motion, and the heavy and the light, or passive and active, from motion toward the center and away from the center. And furthermore, as regards nobility of character in action and conduct, this study above all others can render men clear-sighted, through the likeness, good order, proportion, and freedom from arrogance contemplated in divine things, making those who follow it lovers of this divine beauty, and habituating and as it were reforming them to a like state of soul.

Let us ourselves, then, continually strive to increase this love of the contemplation of things that are always and identically constituted,

f. 0018 · p. 8

by learning what has already been grasped of such mathematical subjects by those who have approached them genuinely and inquiringly, and by intending ourselves also to contribute as great an addition as the time elapsed since them down to our own day could well provide. And whatever we think has now come to light for us, we shall try to set down in writing as briefly as possible, and in such a way that those who have already made some progress can follow, setting forth in proper order, for completeness of the treatise, everything useful for contemplating the heavens; yet, to avoid an overly long account, merely passing over what the ancients established with precision, while working out as best we can whatever was either not grasped at all or not as usefully as possible.
