Chapter 3 · That the Heavens Move as a Sphere¶

Almagest Book I, Chapter 3 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 10–14)¶

It is reasonable that the ancients arrived at their first notions concerning these matters from some such observation as this: they saw the sun, the moon, and the other stars carried from east to west always along circles parallel to one another, beginning to rise from below, from low down and as it were out of the earth itself, rising gradually to a height, then again coming round proportionally and sinking low until, as though falling into the earth, they utterly vanish, and then again, after remaining some time in invisibility, rising and setting as from another beginning, with these times and moreover the places of their risings and settings recurring in an ordered and generally uniform manner.

Above all, what led them to the spherical notion was the revolution of the ever-visible stars, observed to be circular and revolving about one and the same center; for that point necessarily

f. 0021 · p. 11

became a pole of the celestial sphere, since those closer to it revolve in smaller circles, while those farther away describe larger circles in proportion to their distance, until the distance reaches even those that vanish; and of these too, they saw those near the ever-visible stars remaining in invisibility for a short time, and those farther away proportionally longer; so that at the outset they grasped the aforesaid notion from such facts alone, and then in subsequent inquiry perceived the rest that follows from these, since all phenomena without exception testify against alternative views.

For tell me, if anyone were to suppose that the motion of the stars proceeds in a straight line to infinity, as some have held, what manner could be devised whereby each star would be observed moving daily from the same starting point? For how could the stars turn back when rushing toward infinity? Or how could they turn back without being seen? Or why did they not disappear through gradual diminution of their sizes, instead of on the contrary being seen larger right at their disappearances, yet gradually occulted and as it were cut off by the surface of the earth? Moreover, that they should be kindled out of the earth and again quenched into it would appear altogether among the most absurd of notions. For even if one should concede

f. 0022 · p. 12

that such great order in their magnitudes and numbers, and moreover in their distances, places, and times, comes about thus at random and by chance, and that this whole part of the earth has a kindling nature while that has a quenching nature—or rather, that the same part kindles for some people and quenches for others— and that the same stars happen to be already kindled or quenched for some and not yet for others; even if, I say, someone should concede all these absurdities, what could we say about the ever-visible stars, which neither rise nor set? Or for what reason do those that are kindled and quenched everywhere rise and set, while those that do not suffer this are everywhere always above the earth? For surely the same stars will not always be kindled and quenched for some while never undergoing any of these things for others, when it is altogether clear that the very same stars rise and set for some observers, and do neither for others.

In short, whatever other figure of the motion of the heavens one might suppose besides the spherical, the distances from the earth to the parts of the heavens must necessarily become unequal, wherever and however the earth may be situated, so that both the magnitudes and the mutual intervals of the stars ought

f. 0023 · p. 13

to appear unequal to the same observers during each revolution, as coming to be now at a greater and now at a lesser distance, which is not seen to occur. Indeed, even the fact that their sizes appear greater near the horizons is not caused by the distance being less, but by the exhalation of the moisture surrounding the earth coming between our sight and them, just as objects cast into water appear larger, and the deeper they sink, the larger.

And such considerations also lead us to the spherical notion: that the constructions of horoscopic instruments cannot agree on any other hypothesis than this one alone, and that since the motion of the heavens is unhindered and the easiest to move of all, the circular is the easiest to move of plane figures, and the spherical of solids; and likewise that, since among different figures having an equal perimeter those with more angles are greater, the circle becomes greatest of planes, the sphere of solids, and the heaven greater than all other bodies.

Moreover, one can also set out toward such a conception from certain physical considerations: for instance, that of all

f. 0024 · p. 14

bodies the aether is the finest in its parts and the most uniform, and of uniform bodies the surfaces are uniform, and the only uniform surfaces are the circular among planes and the spherical among solids; since, then, the aether is not plane but solid, it remains that it is spherical. And likewise, that nature has constituted all earthly and corruptible bodies wholly out of curved yet non-uniform figures, whereas all divine bodies in the aether are again out of uniform and spherical ones, since if they were plane or disk-shaped they would not present a circular figure to all observers looking from different places on earth at the same time; for this reason, then, it is reasonable that the aether surrounding them, being of a like nature, is also spherical and on account of its uniformity moves circularly and uniformly.
