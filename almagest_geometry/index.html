The Almagest¶

Μαθηματικὴ Σύνταξις (Syntaxis Mathematica) αʹ — Claudius Ptolemy

Claudius Ptolemy

Table of Contents¶

Composed at Alexandria around 150 CE by Claudius Ptolemy and dedicated to Syrus, the complete sixteen chapters of Book I of the Almagest (Μαθηματικὴ Σύνταξις) constitute the supreme surviving synthesis of classical Greek cosmology, chord trigonometry (with the complete 360-row Table of Chords), and spherical Menelaus geometry (culminating in the Table of Obliquity and right-sphere ascensions). Each chapter provides synchronized reading tracks (English, Greek, Summary, and Notes).

Chapter 3 · That the Heavens Move as a Sphere

It is reasonable that the ancients arrived at their first notions concerning these matters from some such observation as this: they saw the sun, the moon, and the other stars carried from east to west always along circles parallel to one another, beginning to rise from below, from low down and as it were out of the earth itself, rising gradually to a height, then again coming round proportionally and sinking low until, as though falling into the earth, they utterly vanish, and then again, after remaining some time in invisibility, rising and setting as from another beginning, with these times and moreover the places of their risings and settings recurring in an ordered and generally uniform manner.

Chapter 6 · That the Earth Has the Ratio of a Point to the Heavens

Moreover, that the earth also has the ratio of a point to sense perception with respect to the distance to the sphere of the so-called fixed stars, a great proof is that from all parts of it the sizes and distances of the stars at the same times appear equal and similar everywhere, just as observations of the same stars from different climes are not found to disagree in the least. Indeed, this too must be added, that gnomons set up in any part of the earth, and likewise the centers of armillary spheres, have the same effect as the true center of the earth, and preserve the sightings and the revolutions of shadows so completely in agreement with the hypotheses of the phenomena as if they took place through the middle point of the earth itself.
