Table of Contents¶

Composed at Alexandria around 150 CE by Claudius Ptolemy and dedicated to Syrus, the opening fourteen chapters of the Almagest (Μαθηματικὴ Σύνταξις) constitute the supreme surviving synthesis of classical Greek computational geometry, chord trigonometry, and spherical geometry. Each chapter of this Multi-Track Book Codex provides synchronized reading tracks (English, Greek, Summary, and Notes) wired to interactive diagrams.

Chapter 9 · Preliminary Method for Geometric Demonstrations

p. 4 Those who have pursued genuine philosophy, O Syrus, seem to me to have acted very rightly in separating the theoretical part of philosophy from the practical. For even if prior to that separation it happens that the practical turns out to depend on the theoretical, nevertheless one would find great difference between them—not merely because some of the moral virtues can belong to many people even without systematic instruction, whereas it is impossible to attain contemplation of the universe without formal teaching, p. 5 but above all because the greatest benefit there comes from continuous activity in actual affairs, whereas here it comes from steady progress in theoretical theorems.

Chapter 9 · Notes

The Dedication to Syrus & Alexandria in the 2nd Century CE: Ptolemy opens his major treatises (Syntaxis Mathematica, Tetrabiblos, Planisphaerium, Analemma) with dedications to Syrus. Working at or near the Canopus inscription monument (dated to Year 10 of Antoninus Pius, c. 146/147 CE), Ptolemy established the definitive paradigm of mathematical astronomy that dominated Mediterranean, Byzantine, Islamic, and Western European science for fifteen centuries.

Chapter 11 · Notes

Hipparchus vs. Ptolemy on Trigonometric Tables: The earliest historical trigonometric table is attributed to Hipparchus (c. 140 BCE), who reportedly compiled a 12-book treatise on chords. Hipparchus tabulated chords in a circle of radius $R = 3438'$ at $7\frac{1}{2}^\circ$ increments. Ptolemy revolutionized chord calculation by reducing the interval from $7\frac{1}{2}^\circ$ to $0^\circ 30'$ (a fifteen-fold refinement!) and establishing the systematic sexagesimal radius $R = 60^p$.

Greek (1898) · English · Summary · Notes

Chapter 11 · Summary

Table Organization: - Total extent: $0^\circ 30'$ to $180^\circ 00'$ in uniform increments of $0^\circ 30'$ (360 rows, organized into 8 pages of 45 rows each). - Base parameters: Circle circumference $= 360^\circ$, Diameter $D = 120^p$, Radius $R = 60^p$.

Chapter 13 · Notes

Modern spherical trigonometry relies on multiple triangle laws (spherical law of cosines, law of sines, Napier’s rules for right spherical triangles). Throughout Books I, II, and VIII of the Almagest—computing solar declinations (I.14), right ascensions (I.16), ascensional differences, oblique ascensions, length of daylight, meridian shadows, and rising/setting stars (Book II)—Ptolemy uses only a single spherical theorem: the six-quantity Menelaus transversal configuration of Chapter 13 (Menelaus, Sphaerica III.1).
