Chapter 8 · That There Are Two Different Primary Motions in the Heavens¶

Almagest Book I, Chapter 8 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 26–30)¶

For these hypotheses, which must necessarily be assumed beforehand for the detailed expositions and those that follow from them, it will suffice to have sketched them in outline thus far, since they will be completely confirmed and corroborated by the very agreement with the phenomena of what is subsequently demonstrated in order. In addition to these, one might rightly consider it worth assuming this general principle as well, that there are two different kinds of primary motions in the heavens: one by which all things are carried from east to west, always making their revolution uniformly and at the same speed along circles parallel to one another, clearly described around the poles of this sphere that carries everything evenly around, whose greatest circle is called the equator because it alone is always bisected by the horizon, a great circle, and because the sun’s revolution along it produces equinox to sense perception everywhere; and the other, according to which

f. 0037 · p. 27

the spheres of the stars make certain motions opposite to the aforementioned movement around different poles, not those of the first revolution. And we suppose these things to be so because from daily observation everything in the heavens without exception is seen to make its risings, culminations, and settings along paths uniform with and parallel to the equinoctial circle, this being peculiar to the first motion; whereas from subsequent and more continuous observation all the other stars appear to preserve for the most part both their mutual intervals and their proper places in the first motion, while the sun, the moon, and the wandering stars make certain varied and mutually unequal passages, yet all toward the eastward and lagging parts relative to the general motion of those stars that preserve their mutual intervals and are carried around as if by a single sphere.

Now if this passage of the wandering stars also took place along circles parallel to the equator—that is, around the poles that produce the first revolution—it would have been sufficient to suppose one

f. 0038 · p. 28

and the same revolution of all things following the first; for thus it would have seemed plausible that their passage took place by different fallings-behind and not by an opposite motion. As it is, however, together with their eastward passages they are always seen to shift toward the north and toward the south, nor is the magnitude of such displacement observed to be uniform, so that this occurrence might seem to happen to them through certain thrusts—yet irregular on such a supposition, while ordered as produced by a circle inclined to the equator; whence such a circle is apprehended as one and the same and proper to the wandering stars, defined and as it were drawn by the motion of the sun, while traversed also by the moon and the planets, which always revolve about it and never at random depart from the limit of displacement marked off along it for each on either side. And since this circle too is observed to be a great circle, because the sun goes equally far north and south of the equator, and since, as we said, the eastward passages of all the planets take place about one and the same circle, it was necessary to assume this second kind of general motion, occurring about the poles

f. 0039 · p. 29

of the inclined circle thus grasped and in the opposite direction to the first motion.

If now we conceive the great circle described through the poles of both the aforementioned circles—which necessarily bisects both the equator and the circle inclined to it at right angles— there will be four points of the inclined circle: two diametrically opposite formed by the equator, called equinoctial, of which the one passing from south to north is called vernal, and the opposite one autumnal; and two formed by the circle through both sets of poles, also clearly diametrically opposite, called solstitial, of which the one south of the equator is called winter and the one to the north summer.

Now the one first motion that encloses all the others will be conceived as circumscribed and as it were bounded by the great circle through both sets of poles, which revolves and carries all else with it from east to west around the poles of the equator, fixed as it were upon the so-called meridian, which differs from the aforementioned circle in this alone,

f. 0040 · p. 30

that it is not always drawn through the poles of the inclined circle as well, and furthermore because it is continuously conceived at right angles to the horizon it is called the meridian, since such a position bisects both the hemisphere above the earth and that below the earth and marks off the midpoints of days and nights.

And the second, manifold motion, enclosed by the first yet enclosing the spheres of all the planets, carried along by the aforementioned motion as we said, yet revolving in the opposite direction around the poles of the inclined circle—which, always fixed upon the circle making the first circumscription, that is, the circle through both sets of poles, naturally revolve along with it and throughout the opposite motion of the second movement always maintain the same position of the inclined great circle described through it relative to the equator.
