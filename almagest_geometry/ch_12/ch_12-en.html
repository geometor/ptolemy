Chapter 12 · On the Arc Between the Tropics¶

Almagest Book I, Chapter 12 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 64–68)¶

Meridian Instruments for Measuring Solar Zenith Distance (Heiberg I, pp. 64–67)¶

p. 64 Before computing spherical tables, one single observational datum is required: the inclination (λόξωσις) of the oblique zodiacal circle to the celestial equator. Ptolemy describes two meridian instruments:

  1. The Graduated Bronze Meridian Armillary Ring (pp. 64–65): A bronze ring, accurately turned and divided into $360$ degrees and sub-divisions, mounted firmly in the meridian plane. An inner concentric ring with two diametrically opposed sight prisms is rotated until the solar beam passes through both sights at solar noon.

  2. The Meridian Quadrant Plinth (pp. 66–67): A stone or wooden block with a flat vertical face aligned precisely along the meridian plane, bearing a quadrant arc centered on a horizontal cylindrical peg. The shadow cast by the peg across the graduated quadrant at local noon gives the solar zenith distance with superior stability.

The Observational Result & Obliquity of the Ecliptic (Heiberg I, pp. 67–68)¶

p. 67 Over multiple years of observation at Alexandria, observing both the summer solstice (when the Sun reaches its highest meridian altitude) and the winter solstice (when it reaches its lowest), Ptolemy determines the distance between the two tropics:

p. 68 “From these observations we find that the arc between the tropics always exceeds $47\frac{2}{3}^\circ$ ($47^\circ 40'$) and is less than $47\frac{3}{4}^\circ$ ($47^\circ 45'$). From this rises very nearly the same ratio as that of :person:`Eratosthenes <eratosthenes>`, which :person:`Hipparchus <hipparchus>` also used: for the arc between the tropics comes to $11$ parts out of $83$ of the meridian circle.”

Since $\frac{11}{83} \times 360^\circ = 47^\circ 42' 39.03\dots'' \approx 47^\circ 42'40''$, bisecting gives Ptolemy’s canonical obliquity of the ecliptic:

$$\varepsilon = 23^\circ 51' 20'', \qquad 2\varepsilon = 47^\circ 42' 40'', \qquad \operatorname{crd}(2\varepsilon) \approx 48;31,55^p.$$
