Chapter 9 · Preliminary Method for Geometric Demonstrations¶

Almagest Book I, Chapters 1–9 (Heiberg 1898, Vol. I.1, pp. 4–31)¶

Chapter 1 · Preface to Syrus (Heiberg I, pp. 4–7)¶

p. 4 Those who have pursued genuine philosophy, O Syrus, seem to me to have acted very rightly in separating the theoretical part of philosophy from the practical. For even if prior to that separation it happens that the practical turns out to depend on the theoretical, nevertheless one would find great difference between them—not merely because some of the moral virtues can belong to many people even without systematic instruction, whereas it is impossible to attain contemplation of the universe without formal teaching, p. 5 but above all because the greatest benefit there comes from continuous activity in actual affairs, whereas here it comes from steady progress in theoretical theorems.

Hence we deemed it fitting on the one hand to regulate our own actions amidst the impressions of daily life so as never to forget, even in small matters, the consideration of beautiful and well-ordered disposition, and on the other hand to devote the greatest part of our leisure to the instruction of theorems—many and noble as they are, yet chiefly those expressly called mathematical. For Aristotle quite fittingly divides the theoretical part too into three primary genera (Metaphysics E.1, 1026a18): the physical, the mathematical, and the theological.

p. 6 For since all existing things have their being from matter, form, and motion—none of which can be seen in actual existence apart from a substrate, though each can be thought without the others—one would reasonably suppose the first cause of the first motion of the universe, if taken simply, to be an invisible and motionless deity, and the kind of science that seeks it out in the highest regions of the cosmos to be theological; whereas the science that investigates material and ever-changing quality (found in white and hot, sweet and soft, and things of that sort) one would call physical, since such substance dwells for the most part among corruptible things below the lunar sphere; while the kind of science declarative of quality with respect to forms and local motions—seeking figure, number, and magnitude, and moreover place, time, and the like—one may define as mathematical.

For such an essence falls, as it were, in the middle between the other two: not only because it can be conceived both through the senses and apart from the senses, and furthermore because it is an attribute of practically all beings universally, both mortal and immortal, changing ever together with those things that alter according to their inseparable form, while preserving unchanged the eternity of the ethereal nature through immovable forms; p. 7 seeing, then, that the other two genera of the theoretical would seem to be grasped more by conjecture than by scientific apprehension—the theological because of its completely invisible and ungraspable nature, the physical because of the unstable and obscure character of matter—so that philosophers could never hope to agree about them, mathematics alone, if one approaches its inquiry rigorously, can furnish reliable and unshakable knowledge to those who practice it, because its demonstration proceeds through indisputable paths: arithmetic and geometry.

Chapters 2–8 · Summary of the Cosmic Hypotheses (Heiberg I, pp. 7–30)¶

Before entering the geometric calculus of chords, Ptolemy establishes in Chapters 2–8 the observational and geometric hypotheses governing spherical astronomy:

  • I.2 — Order of the Treatise (pp. 7–10): Why the general relation of the whole Earth to the whole heaven must precede special demonstrations of solar, lunar, and planetary motions.

  • I.3 — That the Heaven Rotates Spherically (pp. 10–14): Circumpolar stars revolve in parallel circles around a fixed visible pole P, rising and setting stars trace uniform circular paths across the horizon cHOR, and the sphere uniquely encloses the greatest volume among isoperimetric solids.

  • I.4 — That the Earth Too Is Sensibly Spherical as a Whole (pp. 14–16): Lunar eclipses occur at identical absolute moments yet at earlier local hours to western observers and later local hours to eastern observers in exact proportion to longitudinal distance; advancing northward raises the altitude of the pole P.

  • I.5 — That the Earth Is in the Middle of the Heavens (pp. 16–20): Only at the center O of the celestial sphere cABGD does the horizon plane cHOR bisect the zodiacal circle cECL so that six signs always appear above the Earth and equinox shadows lie along a straight east–west line.

  • I.6 — That the Earth Has the Ratio of a Point to the Heavens (pp. 20–21): Gnomon sights and armillary rings on Earth’s surface act as if stationed at the very center O, and the horizon always bisects the celestial sphere.

  • I.7 — That the Earth Makes No Motion of Translation (pp. 21–26): Physical and kinematic arguments placing the stationary Earth at the cosmic center O.

  • I.8 — That There Are Two Different Primary Motions in the Heavens (pp. 26–30): First, the diurnal rotation carrying all things from east to west parallel to the celestial equator ΑΓ around the poles P and P'; second, the counter-rotation of the Sun, Moon, and planets along the oblique circle of the zodiac (ΒΔ) inclined to the equator by the obliquity ε.

Chapter 9 · Preliminary Method for Individual Demonstrations (Heiberg I, pp. 30–31)¶

p. 30 The general introductory outline may be considered to have set forth in summary fashion the matters that required preliminary hypothesis. Since we are now to begin the specific demonstrations—of which we deem the first to be that through which we determine the sizes of straight lines in a circle—we shall first set out a concise method, easily handled with linear demonstration, for calculating their magnitudes, so that we may not, p. 31 by continually lingering over the same topic throughout the entire treatise, be hindered from apprehending what follows.
